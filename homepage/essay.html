Beyond the Walls of Assumption

I still recall the humid summer afternoon in Manhattan, standing near the 59th Street subway entrance, when a woman stopped me for directions. She had that hurried city look, a black coffee in one hand and her phone glued to the other. “You must live around here,” she said after I guided her. I smiled as I admitted that I was from the Bronx. She was astonished by my reassurance and paused before a practiced “Ohh...” flickered across her face. She hid it, but I saw the calculation, the curiosity laced with a kind of nervous distance. In her eyes, I became something else, familiar but foreign.

People always seem to know a Bronx of their own. To them, it’s a place where fire escapes are more than decoration, windows are barricades, and sirens replace birdsong. The news tells gruesome stories of broken glass and gang colors flashing in shadowed alleys. Sometimes, people mutter about that neighborhood off Jerome Avenue, where they say a fight turned into a shooting last fall. Even I tremble thinking about the screeching cars at three in the morning, the sounds of conflicting strangers every night, the banging on the roof of my apartment every few hours, or the sign of murder marked by a painted white bike.

Yet, the Bronx sings its humble melody. It sings at the bodegas, where Spanish, Bengali, and English dialect trip over each other like old friends. It sings at basketball courts, crackling through fences after sundown, and at the laughter of the children on the swings. It has its own rhythm, composed of the clatter of trains and the echoes of prayer above the sound of honking horns. Here, the brick buildings hide not drugs being sold but pockets of warmth: carpet stores, halal butchers, and Bengali restaurants.

Wander down the streets of Starling Avenue, and you’ll see the marks of patterned footprints from decades of Chaad Raat, the night before Eid. Under the soft glow of the string lights, people of all ages zigzagged through bustling crowds, weaving their way through the rows of henna stalls and the nearby racks of shimmering clothes run by the watchful uncles. Deep bass from the speakers vibrated through the avenues, and fireworks painted the dark sky. Nearby, parents huddled close together, talking about the year’s challenges and joys while keeping an eye on the children, eager to break free and join the older kids in the overflowing crowd. My friends and I would dance through the streets, licking the tangy, crispy fuchka off our fingers as the silhouettes of childhood laughter swirled around us. Those nights never felt dark, with the golden star and crescent as our sun.

Where outsiders see conflict and violence, I see groups of teenagers running across public fields playing a game of kabaddi. They are the same teenagers who stay late to clean the masjid floors. Your word for this is “trouble,” while we see it as “community.” In my Bronx, the loudest sounds aren’t sirens but the laughter of children that grows louder after dusk. You call my Bronx dirty, dangerous, and divided. I know it as diverse, resilient, and united. You see exclusion; we live transformation. My Bronx breathes in every language spoken over shop counters, every prayer whispered on woven carpet, and every handshake between hijabs and Yankees caps. There is no monster of the Bronx except the one invented in your foolish minds.

When we step forth from the Harlem River, our skin turns alienated. We become the bizarre that no one can unpuzzle. Our steps become unmatched; you refuse to view us as equals, leaving us at the edge of the table. For once, could you pause and listen to our melody? When the ignorant make us feel unwelcome, when Manhattan glances turn cold, we always fall back on what we know: our shared rhythm, our faith, and our muscle memory of belonging. We return to the neighborhood’s pulse and relax in its arms. The aroma of samosas drifts into the crisp evening air. Families stand together or on prayer rugs, while the young ones play Ludo in the rooms still warm from shared meals. Here, dispassionate glances are replaced by eager eyes from old friends. We nod with empathy before the other can utter a sentence.

Everything here evokes community, built action by action. The store owners lower their gates for Friday prayer, and the cashiers trust you’ll return when you forget your wallet. On Eid mornings, the sidewalks of my neighborhood overflow with Muslims of all ages, bowing their heads to the city’s rough asphalt. Here, the belonging is physical; it's shoulder to shoulder, forehead to ground, and remembered in every muscle and memory. Take an evening stroll down the place locals call “Bangla Bazaar,” where window lights spill onto the sidewalk, and the sounds of radios pulse between Bollywood and Quran recitations. Here, families gather to share food and stories, and shopkeepers greet neighbors by name. Tell them yours, and they will remember it too.

Sometimes I think about the woman I met near the 59th Street Station and feel pity for her. However, it was not her fault that this city cast a spell on her. If she had the chance to step forth onto our asphalt grounds and fall into our embrace, would she have apologized then? We don’t blame you for being afraid, but within your beastly image of us, there is still a heart that beats fondly. The day this city decides to hear our melody will be the day our boroughs unite.
